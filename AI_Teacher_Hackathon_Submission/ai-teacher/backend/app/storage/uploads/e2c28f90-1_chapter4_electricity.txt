Chapter 4: Electricity

Section 4.1: Electric Current
Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor. It is measured
in amperes (A). Current flows from the positive terminal of a source to the negative terminal
through an external circuit, conventionally, even though electrons physically move the
opposite way. A simple analogy: current is like water flowing through a pipe, where the
amount of water passing a point per second is like the current.

Section 4.2: Voltage
Voltage, or potential difference, is the "push" that drives current through a circuit. It is
measured in volts (V). Using the water analogy, voltage is like the water pressure — higher
pressure pushes more water through the pipe for a given pipe width.

Section 4.3: Resistance
Resistance opposes the flow of current in a conductor and is measured in ohms (Ω). Thin wires,
long wires, and certain materials (like nichrome) have higher resistance than thick, short,
highly conductive wires (like copper). In the water analogy, resistance is like a narrow or
rough section of pipe that slows the water down.

Section 4.4: Ohm's Law
Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage
across it and inversely proportional to its resistance: I = V / R. This means that if
resistance increases while voltage stays constant, current must decrease — not increase, a
common misconception among beginners who assume "more resistance means more current pushing
through harder."

Section 4.5: Series and Parallel Circuits
In a series circuit, components are connected end-to-end so the same current flows through
all of them, and the total resistance is the sum of each component's resistance. In a parallel
circuit, components are connected across the same two points, so the voltage across each is
the same, and adding more parallel branches decreases the overall resistance of the circuit.

Chapter 5: Magnetism

Section 5.1: Magnetic Fields
A magnetic field is produced around any current-carrying conductor. The strength and direction
of this field can be found using the right-hand rule: if you point your right thumb in the
direction of conventional current flow, your curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic
field around the wire.

Section 5.2: Electromagnets
Wrapping a current-carrying wire into a coil around an iron core concentrates the magnetic
field and creates an electromagnet, whose strength increases with more coil turns and higher
current.
